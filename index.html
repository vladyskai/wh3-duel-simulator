Finding your way

This page is one endless canvas. Drag to move, scroll or pinch to zoom, or use Jump to at the top right. Change anything and the whole canvas follows at once. Model inputs (top right) lists the numbers the model really needs. The map below runs every step from top to bottom: the left column is A hitting B, the right column (from stage 3) is B hitting A, and they meet in the race at the bottom. Each step's explanation sits to the left of its band.

Reading the pictures

The vertical axis is always a chance. Bars are whole numbers: the height of a bar is the chance of that exact value, and all the bars add up to 100%. A flat block is a value spread evenly over a range: its height is the chance per small step (per 0.1, per 1% or per damage point), so its area, steps times height, is its chance. An arrow is one exact value: it has no width, so its chance is written beside it instead of read on the axis. The pills with a dashed line are the stats, joined to the arrow that uses them.

Unit against unit

Each side is now a whole unit: many soldiers in a formation. This part is a simulation, not exact maths: the page fights the battle many times, swing by swing, with a rule measured in the game.

"Fight them again" plays new fights with the same stats: the numbers wobble a little, as they would in the game.

The rule

What is simplified

  • The rule was measured on Empire Swordsmen and four variants of them (60 and 160 soldiers, +15 attack and defence, twice the health), 10 to 40 files wide. For other units it is an extrapolation.
  • Fights go to the last soldier. No morale: nobody wavers or routs.
  • No charge, no fatigue, no experience. Both units stand face to face from the first second.
  • No flank or rear attack, apart from the wrap of a wider unit, which rests on 6 measured fights.
  • One target per swing: no splash attacks. No bonus against infantry or large targets. No terrain.
  • One soldier per model: no cavalry, monsters or single entities.

How close it is to the game

Against 60 logged fights in 11 match-ups: the winner is right every time, the length of the fight is within 20%, the wounds carried by the living within about 10%. The winner's soldiers left are within 25%, usually on the low side (the model's winner keeps 10 to 20% fewer soldiers than the game's). Details: docs/unit_battle_research.md, check/unit_rule_check.mjs.

1. One swing is a weighted coin

Every swing hits or misses. Only the gap between the attacker's attack and the defender's defence matters.

Formula

A probability cannot leave the range 0 to 1, hence the min and max. The game itself keeps the hit chance between 8% and 90% (confirmed by Creative Assembly), not applied here yet.

2. Damage of one hit

Until stage 4 there is no armour, so every hit does exactly its damage per hit. Nothing is random here yet.

2. Damage per hit is random

Armour blocks a random share of the damage on every hit.

Armour-piercing damage always lands in full. Armour blocks a random share of the base damage on every hit.

The chart is the chance that one hit does exactly that much damage. Dashed lines mark the averages. The pictures to the right follow one hit from the armour roll U to the damage D: each arrow is one operation of the formula, with the numbers of the current stats.

Formula

U is the armour roll: each hit, armour blocks between half and all of its value, as a percentage of the base damage. Above 100 armour the block is capped at 100%, so some hits lose their whole base damage. The game rounds the parts separately, at the end, and there is no minimum damage. These rules come from Creative Assembly's article Feature Focus #2: Damage (2023).

With armour up to 100, the base part before rounding is spread evenly between ℓ = B(1 − armour/100) and h = B(1 − armour/200). Each whole number j gets the share of that range which rounds to it. Example: 9 base, 1 AP against 100 armour. The base part lies between 0 and 4.5, so the hit does 1 damage with chance 1/9 and 2, 3, 4 or 5 with chance 2/9 each. Before stage 5 the AP part is 0.

The average before rounding. Rounding moves it by less than half a point.

3. Hits needed

Every hit does the same damage, so the hits needed is a plain number: divide the hit points by the damage and round up. Hit points and damage only count through this one number.

3. Hits needed is now random too

Keep adding random hits until they reach the enemy's hit points. The count of hits is no longer a fixed number but a distribution.

To the right: the total after k hits is a pile of possible totals, and the part of the pile past the hit points (in colour) is the chance the enemy is dead by hit k. The differences between those chances give the hits needed. The three totals shown are the ones around the middle of the fight.

Formula

The sum of k random hits has its own distribution: the convolution of k copies of one hit's distribution. Because every hit does a whole number of damage, the page computes it exactly, one hit at a time: it tracks the chance of having taken 0, 1, 2 ... damage so far, and whatever reaches the hit points on hit k is P(K = k). With no armour every hit is the same and this falls back to K = HP / D rounded up.

Sk(s) is the chance that after k hits the defender has taken exactly s damage and is still standing (s below the hit points).

4. Swings needed: a negative binomial

Flip the hit coin until it has come up "hit" K times. The number of flips this takes is a negative binomial. Dashed lines mark the means.

4. Swings needed: a mixture

For each possible number of hits, the swings follow a negative binomial. The real distribution blends them, weighted by how likely each number of hits is. Dashed lines mark the means.

To the right: the negative binomials for the three most likely numbers of hits, then every curve shrunk by the chance of its k, then all of them added up.

Formula

The last swing is always a hit: before it, K − 1 hits are spread among n − 1 swings in any order, hence the binomial coefficient.

The variance splits in two (the law of total variance): the luck of hitting or missing, plus the luck of the armour rolls. The hit-or-miss coin and the armour roll are independent.

5. Swings become a time

The first swing lands at t = 0, then one more every attack interval. A slower soldier's curve is stretched to the right.

Formula

a is A's attack interval, b is B's. Swing 1 lands at t = 0, swing n at t = a(n−1).

6. The race

For each moment A could land its killing blow, take the chance that B has not finished yet. Add them up.

Formula

m(n) is how many swings B has made by the time A's n-th swing lands. Only the ratio a/b appears, so the two intervals decide the winner only through their ratio.

Both die only when B swings at exactly the same instant and that swing is B's killing blow.

7. How long the fight lasts

The fight ends at the first killing blow. It is still going at time t only if both soldiers are still standing.

Formula

The two soldiers' luck is independent, so the chances multiply.
