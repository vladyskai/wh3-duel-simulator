Unit against unit

Two units in line against each other. Only the front line fights, and the ranks behind step in as it falls. Three tools answer who wins: two fast approximations and a soldier-by-soldier simulation that referees them.

How the answer is computed

1. Each soldier's kill rate

The duel model already knows how many swings one soldier needs, on average, to kill one fresh enemy. Turned into kills per second, that is the only number the fast tools need from each side.

Formula

a is the attack interval and N the swings needed for one kill, from the duel model. This is renewal theory: if each kill takes a·E[N] seconds on average, kills arrive at one per a·E[N] seconds in the long run. m is the number of soldiers one swing can target (splash). Each of them takes 1/m of the damage and rolls its own hit, as players describe the game's splash attacks.

2. Lanchester's equations: the average fight

Each side loses soldiers at the enemy's kill rate times the number of enemies fighting, which is the front line until fewer are left. Solid lines are this smooth, luck-free fight. Dashed lines are the average of the simulated fights.

Formula

F is the front line: the unit's soldiers divided by its ranks, rounded up. Two extreme cases have exact answers.

This is the square law: strength is kill rate times numbers squared. Twice the soldiers are worth four times the quality, because the extra soldiers all fight.

Both fronts stay full for the whole fight (deep ranks): each side loses soldiers at a constant rate, and this never changes:

This is the linear law: numbers only count once, because the extra soldiers wait behind. Real fights start near the linear law and end near the square law, once a front line can no longer be filled. The curves never quite reach zero, so a side counts as beaten below half a soldier. Lanchester ignores luck, so it calls close fights with false certainty, and it treats a single monster as a smooth fraction of a soldier.

3. A Markov chain: the odds

Follow only the head counts. From any state, the next death is on side A or side B, with chances set by the kill rates. Adding up all the paths gives the probability of every ending.

Formula

This plain chain assumes every waiting time is exponential: memoryless, as if a soldier half-way to a kill were no closer to it. Real kills vary much less, so the plain chain makes fights look closer than they are.

The correction: count each soldier as c small steps (Erlang stages), each c times as fast. A kill keeps its average time, and its variance shrinks by c. Picking c from the real spread of the time to kill makes the chain's luck match the duel model's:

4. The simulation: the referee

Every soldier, every swing, every hit point, with the rules above. It is slow, so it runs a few hundred fights after each change. With one soldier per side and no stagger it gives exactly the duel page's answers (checked by the tests).

Rules

Each soldier swings once per attack interval, starting at a random moment inside its first interval (a real unit does not swing in unison). It targets one enemy, either keeping it until it dies or picking a random living enemy each swing. With splash m, the swing also targets m−1 other random enemies, and each takes 1/m of the damage. Damage does not carry over from a dead soldier to the next.

Only the front line swings and can be hit. When a front soldier dies, the next soldier from behind steps in at once (in the game they take a moment to walk forward). Each unit keeps its own front width: if one front is wider, its extra soldiers are assumed to wrap round the enemy's flank and fight too.

How the game's soldiers really pick their targets is not documented anywhere I could find, so both rules are offered.

Ranks deep: the game's land_units table gives each unit a default formation depth (rank_depth), 4 to 8 ranks for most infantry, so a 160-soldier unit 8 deep has a front of 20. That only the front rank fights is an assumption. A single monster gets surrounded in the game, so give the infantry facing it 1 rank.
